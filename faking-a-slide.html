Faking a Slide

Fake a hand discard of a 1 while holding 12356, and you can make your 4–7 wait look safe.
Who uses this little trick, and how often? I explored M.League and Tenhou data to find out.

7 September 2026EnglishLog analysisDiscard readingTenhouM.League

In short
  • Draw a 1, 2, 8 or 9 and you can discard its twin from your hand instead — the discard then reads as a slide, and your wait looks safe.
  • The chance comes once in 708 hands on Tenhou; when it does, more than a quarter of players take it. In M.League, 12 of 28.
  • It works in one shape only: the 1 from 12356 changes nothing, the 2 from 23467 wins by ron 2.9pt more often. Same lie, different tile.

Fake Hand Discards

A draw discard (tsumogiri; ツモ切り) means discarding the same tile you have just drawn. A hand discard (tedashi; 手出し), by contrast, means discarding a tile that was already in your hand.

In online mahjong, the other players can generally tell whether a discard is a draw discard or a hand discard. The distinction is also usually visible in in-person mahjong unless a player uses kotegaeshi — a quick hand-shuffling technique that is often explicitly prohibited in professional games.

When you draw a duplicate of a tile already in your hand and want to discard one of the two copies, you have a choice. You can simply discard the newly drawn tile, making a draw discard, or you can discard the copy that was already in your hand. The latter action is called a fake hand discard (karagiri; 空切り). Either way, your resulting hand is identical, but your opponents receive different information depending on how you make the discard.

In many situations, making a fake hand discard is a mistake because it reveals information unnecessarily. Your opponents will not know whether the hand discard was “fake” — made after drawing a duplicate — or genuine — made after drawing a different tile. Nevertheless, the hand discard itself reveals that you had been holding that tile until that turn. Even such a small piece of information can sometimes prove costly. Under the right circumstances, it may allow an experienced player to draw surprisingly accurate conclusions about your hand.

To see this kind of deduction in action, watch the video below, in which Nakabayashi reviews the online replay of an M.League game he played earlier that day and explains his thought process.[1]

Nakabayashi reads Sarukawa's karagiri He reconstructs the hand and names the wait exactly. One fake discard can give away that much. YouTube · from 1:20

During the stream, Nakabayashi explains that, while playing, he noticed Sarukawa — the player to his right — make an “unnatural” hand discard. Combining this observation with information from previous turns, he recognized it as a fake hand discard. From there, he was able to deduce which tiles were safe and which were dangerous. As it turned out, all of his deductions were correct.

I hope the video gives you a sense of Nakabayashi’s thought process and the logical steps behind his conclusions. Even among expert players, deductions this accurate are probably rare. Still, there is no reason to reveal information through a fake hand discard when doing so serves no purpose.

Slide Discards

We distinguish one more type of hand discard: the slide discard (slide-giri, or simply slide; スライド切り). A slide discard occurs when you draw a tile and discard its suji tile, shifting an existing sequence toward the centre of the suit.

Recall that tiles closer to the terminals are generally safer to discard and reveal less information to your opponents. For example, discarding a 5 reveals that both the 2–5 and 5–8 suji are safe against your hand. Discarding a 2, by contrast, does not reveal that the 5–8 suji is safe.

Suppose, for example, that your hand contains and you draw . You might keep the and discard from your hand, thereby sliding the sequence from to . If you later draw a red , you can then discard and slide again, keeping the red . Similarly, if you have 789 and draw 6, you might discard 9, sliding from 789 to 678.[2] Unlike a fake hand discard, a slide discard is often both useful and innocuous.

That said, a slide discard can sometimes reveal information about your hand, especially when a 1, 2, 8, or 9 pushed out by the slide appears “unnatural” to an expert hand reader. An opponent may be able to use that information to their advantage.

In the video below, from an M.League game played earlier that day, Nakabayashi pushed aggressively against Date’s riichi with striking confidence. Viewers wondered why he had been willing to take such a risk. He explains that he had — correctly, as we found out later — inferred that Date’s discard was a slide discard.

From this, he concluded that she was likely holding and that was therefore relatively safe.[3] He continued pushing and eventually won a mangan hand. The individual steps in his reasoning are logical and plausible.

It is important, however, not to overstate the range of situations in which this kind of read is useful. In the video, Nakabayashi himself emphasizes the limitations of hand-reading techniques, noting that his inferences are useless nine times out of ten. This is not necessarily because the inferences are inaccurate. Even an accurate inference may be of no practical use when your hand is either too weak or too strong.

Suppose you infer that is safer than other tiles against an opponent’s riichi. If your hand is three tiles away from tenpai and has very little value, knowing that is relatively safe will not help much. You should be discarding completely safe tiles anyway.

Hand reading also involves considerable guesswork. It relies on assumptions that may or may not be correct — for example, that your opponents are making reasonable decisions intended to maximize their chances of winning the hand. That assumption can easily fail if a player makes a mistake, is defending against someone else, or has some other objective. Even experienced players sometimes make careless mistakes or seemingly “unreasonable” choices.

At the other extreme, if your hand is extremely valuable and already in tenpai, you may decide to discard a tile even if your reading suggests that it is highly dangerous. The primary purpose of hand reading is not to identify an opponent’s exact wait but to improve your push/fold decisions.

Relatedly, although a slide discard may reveal some information, its benefits usually outweigh its costs. First, your opponents may not be paying attention to whether your discard came from your hand or was the tile you just drew. Even if they notice, they may not have the necessary knowledge of tile logic to draw a useful inference. In such cases, the informational cost is negligible.

Second, and more importantly, slide discards often provide meaningful benefits in tile acceptance, hand value, and defense — unlike fake hand discards. If you have and draw , refusing to slide by discarding may force you to discard a red if you draw one later. Even a regular may be more valuable than , particularly if an opponent is waiting on the 5–8 suji.

In the video, Nakabayashi comments that Date was correct to make the slide discard. By contrast, in the first video discussed above, he describes Sarukawa’s fake hand discard as a clear mistake.

Faking a Slide

We now come to the main topic of this article. In one exceptional situation, drawing a 1, 2, 8, or 9 and discarding the matching copy from your hand may lead an opponent to mistake the discard for a slide. In other words, there are rare situations in which making a fake hand discard with one of these tiles can be beneficial.

This technique is more a piece of trivia than a practical skill. Learning it will probably not make you a significantly stronger player. Nevertheless, many strong players know about it — and know that it is mostly trivia — and some occasionally put it into practice.

Given this discard order, your opponents may conclude that the newly discarded was unlikely to have been an isolated tile. You have 23467 and are waiting on 5–8, then draw 2.

How Often Does It Happen?

To find out, I analyzed all available game records from M.League through the 2025–26 season and from hanchan games played at Tenhou’s Phoenix Table. The datasets contain 93,340 player-hands — equivalent to 23,335 deals — from M.League and 19,102,636 player-hands — equivalent to 4,775,659 deals — from Tenhou.[4]

I extracted every instance in which a player was in tenpai with one of the relevant shapes and then drew the corresponding 1, 2, 8, or 9. Hands in which the player had already declared riichi were excluded: after declaring riichi, the player has no choice but to discard the drawn tile, so a fake hand discard is impossible.

The two shape families produce fake hand discards at nearly identical rates. On Tenhou, players choose to fake a slide in more than one-quarter of the relevant opportunities — that is, when they are in tenpai with one of these shapes and draw the corresponding 1, 2, 8, or 9. They do so more frequently with a closed dama hand (33.1%) than with an open hand (25.5%).

The opportunity itself is rare

Being in tenpai with one of these shapes and then drawing the relevant 1, 2, 8, or 9 is already a rare occurrence — and two entirely separate populations land on almost the same frequency.

All 28 M.League Cases

The shape came up 28 times in M.League play; 12 of them were karagiri. Each row links to the record. (The official viewer animates a hand discard and a draw discard identically, so the motion gives nothing away — though the colour of the discarded tile does distinguish them.)

Go Kobayashi was the first M.League player to fake a slide in this way, doing so during the 2018–19 season. Interestingly, no other player did so for the next five seasons. Some players renowned for their hand-reading abilities — including Nakabayashi, whom I introduced above — have never even had an opportunity to use the tactic.

I first learned about this strategy from a book by Nobuhiro “Black Digital” Ishibashi. Yet even he has never encountered the necessary combination of hand shape and draw in an M.League game.

The list of players who have used the tactic also includes some of the “usual suspects” — players frequently associated with hand reading and psychological play. They include Ken “I’ll Do Everything I Can to Win” Sonoda, Namba “The Demon’s Read” Shibukawa, Kenji “Mahjong IQ 220” Katsumata, and Yu “Freak-Psychometric Mahjong” Suzuki.

At the same time, it seems fitting that some highly successful M.League players, such as Hisato “The Devil” Sasaki, never engage in this kind of deceptive play. This suggests that whether a player uses the tactic has, at most, a marginal effect on their overall performance.

Does It Work?

The purpose of this tactic is to make the 4–7s wait of 12356s — or the 5–8s wait of 23467s — appear safe, thereby encouraging an opponent to push one of the winning tiles. How often do opponents fall for the deception? Only opponents who still have a choice of what to discard can potentially be fooled, so the analysis is restricted to opponents who have not declared riichi.

I compare the frequency with which a player wins by ron on the target suji against a non-riichi opponent, depending on whether the player made a fake hand discard or simply discarded the tile they had just drawn. The Tenhou dataset contains 6,748 relevant instances drawn from approximately 4.7 million deals.

The counts in the two rightmost columns differ slightly — 425 versus 422, and 1,056 versus 1,047 — because in a few cases the player’s wait changed afterward. For example, might become .

Overall, players won by ron on the target suji in 22.8% of cases after making a fake hand discard, compared with 21.4% after making a draw discard. When the results are separated by hand shape, the patterns differ.

  • For the 12356s / 45789s family, the estimated difference is −0.72 percentage points, with a 95% confidence interval of [−4.43, +2.43]. This difference is statistically indistinguishable from zero.
  • For the 23467s / 34678s family, fake hand discards are associated with a 2.89-percentage-point increase in the probability of winning by ron on the target suji. The 95% confidence interval is [+0.04, +5.82] percentage points.

Thus, the results provide some evidence that the tactic works with the 23467s / 34678s family, but not with the 12356s / 45789s family. Because this is observational evidence, however, the difference should not necessarily be interpreted as the causal effect of making a fake hand discard. In a separate analysis that also includes hands not yet in tenpai, the association becomes more pronounced.

Stronger Players Use It More Often

The proportion of relevant opportunities in which players make a fake hand discard rather than a draw discard rises monotonically with rank and rating. This pattern is not driven by a general tendency among stronger players to make more fake hand discards in all situations. Rather, stronger players appear to use them selectively when holding one of the shapes discussed above.

The baseline remains essentially flat across ranks and ratings (18.0% → 17.5% → 18.1%). In other words, stronger players do not simply make more fake hand discards overall; they are more likely to make one specifically when it can imitate a slide and potentially disguise their wait.

Notes

No meaningful differences across years

The analysis pools three years of Tenhou data. Year by year: 2022, 729 of 2,505 opportunities (29.1%); 2023, 662 of 2,529 (26.2%); January–August 2026, 461 of 1,714 (26.9%). The rate is somewhat higher in 2022, but the difference is not large enough to support a meaningful conclusion. Pooling the years also produces more precise breakdowns by rank and rating.

Declaration tiles are separate

In the 64 cases where riichi was declared on this shape, 51 (79.7%) were hand discards. That is not a property of the shape. Counting every discard in Tenhou 2023 where a player discarded a tile of the same kind as the one drawn: 75.6% of riichi declaration tiles are hand discards (8,880 of 11,740), against 8.9% of all other discards (351,904 of 3,938,806). Declaration tiles running eight times higher is a feature of the game at large, not of this lie, so they are excluded from the counts above.
