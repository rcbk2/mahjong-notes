The Schemer’s Downfall

You can fake a slide with a noten hand to make a future wait look safe. But there’s a catch: your deception may end up revealing the truth.

13 September 2026EnglishLog analysisDiscard readingTenhouM.League

TL;DR
  • A fake slide can be done before the hand reaches tenpai.
  • However, the hand may eventually take a different wait, making the 4-7 suji genuinely safe.

Faking a Slide

The previous analysis focused on situations in which a player draws a 1, 2, 8, or 9 while already in tenpai, waiting on 4-7, 3-6, 5-8, or 2-5. The same tactic can be attempted when the hand is still noten. By faking a slide in advance, you may be able to make a future 4-7 wait appear safe. The same principle applies to the corresponding 3-6, 5-8, and 2-5 waits.

Interestingly, the fake-hand-discard rate is generally higher in M.League than on Tenhou, for both tenpai and noten hands. One possible explanation is that M.League games attract more spectators, giving players an additional incentive to attempt subtle, strategically interesting plays. Even when the tactic does not affect the outcome, players may expect commentators to notice it and explain their intentions to the audience. Nobuhiro “Black Digital” Ishibashi, for example, is a regular commentator. Some commentators take pride in identifying such subtle tactics and explaining the reasoning behind choices that might otherwise seem mysterious at first glance. Alternatively, M.League players may expect their opponents to pay closer attention to the distinction between a hand discard and a draw discard.

Does Your Left Player Fall for It?

One reason to fake a slide with a noten hand may be to encourage the left player to discard or , allowing you to call it. Whether this tactic works should be evaluated based on the information actually available to the left player. They cannot see whether your hand really contains 12356; all they see is that a left your hand as a hand discard rather than a draw discard. I examined every first discard of a 1, 2, 8, or 9 by a player who had not declared riichi (75 million discards across three years of Phoenix Table records) and checked whether the left player subsequently discarded either of the two tiles the apparent slide would suggest was safe (e.g., or for a ). I excluded cases in which either target tile was already in the discarder’s pile, since this would make the tiles genbutsu or suji.

At first glance, hand discards are nearly four percentage points more likely than draw discards to be followed by one of the target half-suji tiles. However, hand discards of 1, 2, 8, 9 occur about two turns earlier on average than draw discards. After adjusting the comparison by turn, the observed difference shrinks to just 0.43 percentage points. Given how small the adjusted difference is, there is little evidence that the left player responds to the apparent slide.

It is fascinating to see players with such different styles succeed, demonstrating that there is no single path to success in mahjong.

Conclusion

As I mentioned in the previous article, this tactic is more of a curiosity than an essential piece of knowledge for improving your skills. Still, I hope these two articles serve two purposes. First, paying attention to tactics like this can allow us to enjoy watching M.League matches and Phoenix Table games. Second, this example illustrates the importance of understanding the mechanism behind a rule of thumb rather than blindly following a memorized “formula.” If you simply memorize that you should fake a hand discard of 1 when holding 12356, you might try it with a hand that is still three tiles away from tenpai or against opponents who are not paying attention to the distinction between hand discards and draw discards. As with many mahjong strategies, knowing exceptions (when not to use the tactic) is just as important as knowing the tactic itself.
