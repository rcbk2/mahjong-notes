The Rule of Thumb

Defending against open hands is an essential skill for players who want to progress from the intermediate (≈ Tenhou 4d–6d; Jantama Master 2–Saint 1) to the advanced level (≈ Tenhou 7d+, Saint 2+).1 One challenge is that, unlike riichi hands, open hands provide no definitive indication that they are ready. Genki Hirasawa, a prominent mahjong author and YouTuber, offers the following rule of thumb for estimating the likelihood that an opponent with an open hand is in tenpai:

An open hand has roughly a 50% chance of being in tenpai if:

  • its third call occurs by the end of the first row of the discard pond (turns 1–6);
  • its second call occurs by the end of the second row (turns 7–12); or
  • its first call occurs by the end of the third row (turns 13–18)

Hirasawa mentions this rule several times in his books2 and videos, describing it as something like a folk theorem. My guess is that he encountered supporting statistics somewhere but can no longer locate the source. I therefore wanted to see whether the data support the rule. Note that Hirasawa presents it as a potentially useful baseline estimate, not a definitive statistic. In practice, we would combine it with other information, such as the distribution of middle tiles (3-7) vs terminals and near-terminals (1, 2, 8, and 9), possible imbalances among the suits, the number of hand discards after calls, and other features of the discard pond.

I explored this question using game data from Tenhou and M.League.

Results from Tenhou

The Tenhou dataset covers all East–South games played at the Phoenix Table from 2015 through 2023, along with the first eight and a half months of 2026.3 It contains more than 1.6 million East–South games, 18,034,322 hands (局), and more than 35 million calls. Overall, I would say that the data support the folk theorem. However, the rule leaves many details unspecified. For example, at what point in the first row does the tenpai probability reach 50%? Our analysis helps fill in these details. The following figure summarizes the results.

The curves show the proportion of hands in tenpai among players making their nth call on a given turn. Tenpai is measured immediately after the nth call; that is, when the player discards a tile after calling, even if another player wins on that discard. Having four calls guarantees tenpai, except in the extremely rare situation in which one’s tanki wait tiles have already been used in one of their own open sets. I therefore focus on first, second, and third calls.

The tenpai probability for a player with three calls exceeds 50% as early as turn 4. By the end of the first row, three-call hands are in tenpai about three-quarters of the time. Similarly, the probability for a player with two calls exceeds 50% as early as turn 8.

Results from M.League

The average call rate among M.League players is lower than that among Tenhou players (22.8% versus 33.6%), suggesting that M.League players call more selectively. Accordingly, their probability of being in tenpai after calling is generally higher than that of Tenhou players. For a one-call hand, the probability exceeds 50% during the second row rather than the third row, as specified by the folk theorem. That said, we have only 1,004 observations of hands with a third call, so these results should be treated as a rough reference.

How to Read These Numbers

“The moment of the call”

The discard that follows a pon, chi or open kan — including when that discard deals in. An added kan upgrades an existing pon without exposing a new block, so it does not advance the meld count (536,273 of them, kept separate). A concealed kan leaves the hand closed and is not counted as a meld.

Tenpai ≠ able to ron

Tenpai here is shape tenpai (shanten = 0), matching Tenhou’s end-of-hand ruling: a wait all four of whose tiles are in your own hand is noten, while one whose four tiles are visible elsewhere still counts. An open hand can be tenpai with no yaku and unable to ron, so this is not the same thing as deal-in risk.

The fourth meld

A one-tile hand waiting on a tanki, so it is tenpai almost by construction. The exceptions are hands whose last tile is the fourth copy of a tile they themselves ponned — 5 cases in 5,996.
