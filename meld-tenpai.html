The Rule of Thumb

Defending against open hands is an essential skill for players who want to progress from the intermediate (≈ Tenhou 4d–6d; Jantama Master 2–Saint 1) to the advanced level (≈ Tenhou 7d+, Saint 2+). One challenge is that, unlike riichi hands, open hands provide no definitive indication that they are ready. Genki Hirasawa, a prominent mahjong author and YouTuber, offers the following rule of thumb for estimating the likelihood that an opponent with an open hand is in tenpai:

An open hand has roughly a 50% chance of being in tenpai if:

  • its third call occurs by the end of the first row of the discard pond (turns 1–6);
  • its second call occurs by the end of the second row (turns 7–12); or
  • its first call occurs by the end of the third row (turns 13–18)

Hirasawa mentions this rule several times in his books and videos, describing it as something like a folk theorem. My guess is that he encountered supporting statistics somewhere but can no longer locate the source. I therefore wanted to see whether the data support the rule. Note that Hirasawa presents it as a potentially useful baseline estimate, not a definitive statistic. In practice, we would combine it with other information, such as the distribution of middle tiles (3-7) vs terminals and near-terminals (1, 2, 8, and 9), possible imbalances among the suits, the number of hand discards after calls, and other features of the discard pond.

I explored this question using game data from Tenhou and M.League.

Results from Tenhou

The Tenhou dataset covers all East–South games played at the Phoenix Table from 2015 through 2023, along with the first eight and a half months of 2026.1 It contains more than 1.6 million East–South games, 18,034,322 hands (局), and more than 35 million calls. Overall, I would say that the data support the folk theorem. However, the rule leaves many details unspecified. For example, at what point in the first row does the tenpai probability reach 50%? Our analysis helps fill in these details. The following figure summarizes the results.

The curves show the proportion of hands in tenpai among players making their nth call on a given turn. Tenpai is measured immediately after the nth call; that is, when the player discards a tile after calling, even if another player wins on that discard. Having four calls guarantees tenpai, except in the extremely rare situation in which one’s tanki wait tiles have already been used in one of their own open sets. I therefore focus on first, second, and third calls.

The tenpai probability for a player with three calls exceeds 50% as early as turn 4. By the end of the first row, three-call hands are in tenpai about three-quarters of the time. Similarly, the probability for a player with two calls exceeds 50% as early as turn 8.

Results from M.League

The average call rate among M.League players is lower than that among Tenhou players (22.8% versus 33.6%), suggesting that M.League players call more selectively. Accordingly, their probability of being in tenpai after calling is generally higher than that of Tenhou players. For a one-call hand, the probability exceeds 50% during the second row rather than the third row, as specified by the folk theorem. That said, we have only 1,004 observations of hands with a third call, so these results should be treated as a rough reference.

Hand Discards after Call

Hirasawa goes on to write that we should adjust our estimate of an open-hand opponent’s tenpai probability upward after observing a hand discard following their most recent call.2 Although this seems intuitive, the analysis below shows that it is often not the case.

I treat hands whose first call occurred on turn k (for k = 3, 6, 9, or 12) as a cohort. For each subsequent turn t (t = k + m, …, 18), I then calculate the probability of tenpai after the mth hand discard following the call (for m = 1, 2, or 3). In the figures below, the blue curves show the tenpai probability when the first hand discard occurs on turn t, the yellow curves show it when the second hand discard occurs on turn t, and the red curves show it when the third hand discard occurs on turn t. The gray curves show the baseline tenpai probability on turn t for all hands in the cohort, including those that have not made a hand discard since the call. Observations leave the cohort once the player calls again or the hand ends (by any player’s win or an exhaustive draw). Curves are omitted for cases with fewer than 200 observations.

The baseline probabilities mostly level off after five or six turns because tenpai hands leave the denominator when they win. The saturation level therefore depends on the turn of the most recent call.

Remarkably, the colored curves fall below the gray baseline in many cases. This suggests that, after observing an open-hand player make a hand discard, we should adjust our estimate of their tenpai probability downward rather than upward. For hands with a very early first call (k = 3), the number of subsequent hand discards is mostly noise: the tenpai probabilities differ little through roughly turn 10, and only modestly thereafter. For hands with a later first call (k = 6, 9, or 12), the number of hand discards begins to matter, but in a counterintuitive way. In all three cohorts, a greater number of hand discards between the most recent call and turn t is associated with a lower tenpai probability (m = 1 > m = 2 > m = 3). These differences become larger as k increases. Among hands whose first call occurred on turn 12, those that subsequently made three consecutive hand discards through turn 15 had only a 14.3% chance of being in tenpai, less than one-third of the baseline probability.

The blue curves (m = 1) do appear to cross above the gray baseline toward the end of the hand. As we will see in the next section, however, this is because the baseline probability is not conditioned on the number of previous hand discards.

Figures for n = 2 cohorts

The relationship is even clearer among cohorts defined by a second call. Because the baseline tenpai probabilities are already so high, the probabilities associated with hand discards (m = 1, 2, or 3) never exceed the baseline, except on turn 18 for k = 6. For hands with two calls, the timing of the second call is a stronger signal of tenpai than the number of subsequent hand discards. As long as the player continues to discard from their hand, their tenpai probability remains lower than the baseline.

Conditioning on History

Although the figures above are descriptively accurate, they do not directly answer the question we face while defending: should we adjust our estimate based on whether the opponent makes a hand discard or a draw discard? The hand-discard groups (m = 1, 2, or 3) are restricted to hands with m − 1 previous hand discards following the most recent call, whereas the baseline group has no such restriction. Because the defending player knows how many previous hand discards they have observed, the appropriate comparison is between hand discards and draw discards while controlling for the number of previous hand discards (r). The results are strikingly consistent. In every cohort, turn, and history level examined, hands with a hand discard have a lower probability of being in tenpai than hands with a draw discard. A defender who knows the number of previous hand discards should therefore adjust their estimate of the opponent’s tenpai probability downward after a hand discard and upward after a draw discard. This makes intuitive sense: players who continue making draw discards toward the end of a hand are more likely to have already reached tenpai.

How to Read These Numbers

“The moment of the call”

The discard that follows a pon, chi or open kan — including when that discard deals in. An added kan upgrades an existing pon without exposing a new block, so it does not advance the meld count (536,273 of them, kept separate). A concealed kan leaves the hand closed and is not counted as a meld.

Tenpai ≠ able to ron

Tenpai here is shape tenpai (shanten = 0), matching Tenhou’s end-of-hand ruling: a wait all four of whose tiles are in your own hand is noten, while one whose four tiles are visible elsewhere still counts. An open hand can be tenpai with no yaku and unable to ron, so this is not the same thing as deal-in risk.

The fourth meld

A one-tile hand waiting on a tanki, so it is tenpai almost by construction. The exceptions are hands whose last tile is the fourth copy of a tile they themselves ponned — 5 cases in 5,996.
