GAME RECORDS / TENHOU HOUOU

Staggered Discards of Terminal Pairs

Testing Zundamon’s terminal-pair discard theory against eighteen million hands of Tenhou records.

20 September 2026EnglishLog analysisDiscard readingTenhou

rcbk2The beast that counted tiles at the edge of the world.

TL;DR

  • Only 60.6% of apparent staggered terminal-pair discards come from genuine pairs. A short interval (1–4 turns) with no intervening block discard raises this to about 80%.
  • The theory’s main prediction fails: waits on the first inward suji (2–5 / 5–8) are about 20% more likely after the pattern, not less.
  • Waits on the second inward suji (3–6 / 4–7) are about 5% less likely — too small a difference to act on by itself.
  • The auxiliary prediction holds best: bad-shape waits on 5 are about 20% less likely.

Hand-Discard Reading

I’m a huge fan of Jangoro Zundamon (@M Kiru Channel), who provides critical commentary on M.League games.1 Although the commentary can be quite harsh, Zundamon combines sharp hand reading with a deep understanding of the game. Several professional players, including M.Leaguers Hori and Uchikawa, have publicly praised Zundamon’s insights. I enjoy watching the commentary alongside the game records available here to see how well the reads hold up.

I was therefore excited when Zundamon launched a new series titled “A Course on Hand-Discard Reading” in August 2026. The lectures are introductory and aimed at viewers who have never studied hand-discard reading. In the regular commentary videos, Zundamon often refers to hand-reading theories without explaining them in much detail. The new lecture series, by contrast, walks viewers through the logic behind them.

Zundamon’s Hand-Reading Lecture #1 Zundamon shares their hand-reading techniques, starting from the basics. YouTube

One theory Zundamon occasionally invokes is what I will call “Staggered Discards of Terminal Pair”（端牌の間隔対子落とし、端牌対子の間隔切り, etc.）. The theory appears, for example, in episode 14, episode 19, episode 66, and episode 70.

The theory’s main prediction is that when a player hand-discards a pair of terminal tiles several turns apart — for example, discarding from the hand on turns four and six — the two neighboring suji, – and –, become safer than they would be under ordinary circumstances.

I was fascinated by this theory. Although I have read my fair share of mahjong strategy books, I have never seen it explained in any of them. The logic behind it initially appeared airtight, and the resulting read could be useful on both offense and defense. I therefore wanted to examine the data to see how useful this theory would be in practice — for example, how often this type of discard shows up in actual games, and whether the prediction has boundary conditions.

My analysis of Tenhou game records, however, suggests that the theory’s main prediction is inconsistent with the data. In fact, one of the suji predicted to be safer appears to be more dangerous than the baseline. At the same time, the data support one of the theory’s auxiliary predictions.

Before presenting the results, I will first sketch the logic behind the theory. My explanation is based almost entirely on Zundamon’s lectures — primarily lectures 1 and 2, but also lecture 6 — so readers already familiar with the theory can skip this section. I then divide the theory’s empirical predictions into two parts. The first asks whether staggered discards really indicate that the player originally held a pair. The second asks whether staggered discards actually make the adjacent suji safer.

Zundamon’s Hand-Discard Reading Theory

Definitions

Zundamon begins by arguing that only three types of hand discards (tedashi) convey reliable information. Other hand discards are generally too noisy to serve as useful signals.

1. Reverse discards

  • A player discards a tile from 3 through 7 and later hand-discards a 1, 2, 8, or 9 in any suit.
  • Alternatively, a player discards a 2 or 8 and later hand-discards a 1 or 9 in any suit.
  • Example: the player discards on turn three and later hand-discards .

2. Staggered protorun (taatsu) discards

  • A player discards a number tile on turn t and then, on turn t + 2 or later, hand-discards another tile that would form a protorun with it.
  • Example: the player discards on turn three and hand-discards on turn five.

3. Staggered pair (toitsu) discards

  • A player discards a number tile on turn t and then, on turn t + 2 or later, hand-discards another copy of the same tile.
  • Example: the player discards on turn three and hand-discards another on turn five.

What these three cases have in common is that the second tile was unlikely to have been kept as an isolated tile. In a normal hand — that is, one not pursuing kokushi, chiitoitsu, or a one-suit hand — a player would not normally keep an isolated over an isolated , or keep an isolated after discarding . In these examples, the retained or was therefore probably connected to other tiles.

Most other hand discards are less informative because the discarded tile may have been either isolated or connected. For example, if a player hand-discards on consecutive turns rather than several turns apart, the player may simply have held an isolated pair of in a hand containing three pairs. According to Zundamon, we can therefore concentrate on these three categories rather than trying to remember every hand discard made by all three opponents.2

Exceptions

Several exceptions must be kept in mind. According to Zundamon, however, these situations often produce other visible clues.

A. The second tile was retained as a safety tile. In particular, the player may have drawn the second copy after discarding the first. Suppose the player is in the process of abandoning a tile block, such as a protorun, and then draws a copy of a previously discarded . The player might retain because it appears safer, discard instead, and then hand-discard on the following turn.

B. The second tile was retained as a potential pair for chiitoitsu.

C. The second tile was retained as a floating tile to pursue a yaku, such as sanshoku or ittsu.

Hypotheses

In summary, this process produces the following predictions:

Predictions
  • P1. Conditional on the staggered discards, the first inward suji (2–5) should be less likely to be the wait. The theory ranks this as the safest of the two — the elimination leaves only one pattern (4-b) that produces it.
  • P2. The second inward suji (3–6) should also be less likely, though not as unlikely as the first: one pattern (4-d) plus a corner case (112245) survive the elimination.
  • P3. A bad-shape wait on 5 should also be less likely.

These are stated for staggered hand discards of 1; discards of 9 mirror them, with 5–8 and 4–7 as the corresponding suji.

Empirical Test

Zundamon claims that this theory is more useful than many other hand-discard reading theories but warns that staggered pair discards occur much less frequently than the other two types. I therefore wanted to examine Tenhou game records to estimate how often the pattern occurs and, as a derivative, whether its predictions are supported by the data.

Statistical analysis of game records is particularly well suited to testing hand-reading theories. I am more skeptical of observational studies that attempt to estimate the causal effects of strategic choices on game outcomes — for example, studies comparing the average hand-level expected value (kyoku shūshi, 局収支) of riichi and dama. The problem is that players choose between riichi and dama rather than being randomly assigned to one of them. Moreover, they are more likely to choose riichi when they believe, for reasons that may not be observed or understood by the analyst, that riichi is the better option. Consequently, even if riichi is associated with a higher average hand-level EV, the observed association does not by itself establish that declaring riichi caused the improvement.

Hand-reading theories generally make predictive rather than causal claims. The present question is not what would happen if we forced a player to discard a terminal pair in a staggered fashion. Instead, it is what we can infer about the player’s concealed hand after observing that discard pattern. The player’s endogenous discard choice is therefore not necessarily a source of bias. On the contrary, the relationship between the concealed hand and the player’s discard choice is precisely what makes the discard informative.3

I divide the theory’s observable implications into two components. The first prediction is that staggered discards of a terminal tile usually represent the breakup of a genuine pair. I test this by asking whether the player already held the second copy of the terminal tile when discarding the first. In a later supplementary video, episode 6, Zundamon acknowledges that the original lecture may have overstated this probability. In particular, the signal may be weak when the player discards several number tiles between the first and second terminal-tile discards.

The second component consists of predictions P1–P3 as boxed above — including the ordering claim that the first inward suji should be the safer of the two.

Each prediction must be evaluated relative to an appropriate baseline. My initial baseline consists of other riichi hands without staggered terminal-pair discards. Because producing a staggered discard pattern necessarily takes time, players with such a pattern tend to declare riichi later than other players. I therefore adjust the comparison for the turn on which riichi was declared.4

Findings

The data come from Tenhou’s Phoenix Table. They cover 2015–2023 and January through mid-September 2026, comprising 1,692,981 hanchan (半荘), 18,034,322 hands (局). I currently do not have access to Tenhou data for 2024 or 2025.

First-Stage Results: Did the Player Actually Hold a Pair?

Conditional on observing what appears to be a staggered discard of a terminal pair, how likely is it that the player actually held both copies when making the first discard? The theory depends on the assumption that the player initially held a terminal pair. If the player discarded one copy on turn t while retaining the other for several turns, we may infer that the retained copy was connected to a nearby tile block. That inference, in turn, provides the basis for predicting that the inward suji are safer. If the player did not initially hold both copies, however, we have no reason to expect that the hand has a nearby block or that the inward suji tiles are safer.

Among all observed cases of staggered terminal-tile discards, only 60.6% represented genuine pairs—approximately 1.986 million out of 3.278 million cases. In the remaining cases, the player first discarded an isolated terminal, later drew another copy, retained it for at least one turn, and then hand-discarded it.

As discussed above, a player might retain the second terminal despite its lack of connection to nearby blocks for several reasons. The player may be pursuing a one-suit hand, kokushi, or chiitoitsu. These explanations are generally less plausible after the player has already discarded that terminal, and such hands often produce other visible clues in the discard pond. A more important possibility is that the player retained the terminal as a safety tile. This explanation is more difficult to identify because it may leave few visible clues.

The good news is that several observable features are associated with the probability that the two discards came from a genuine pair. The first is the length of the interval between the discards, as shown in Table 2. When the interval is between one and four,5 approximately three-quarters of the cases involve genuine pairs. When the interval is between five and eight, the proportion falls below one-third. Another useful indicator is whether the player discards a tile block—a taatsu or toitsu—during the interval. When the interval is between one and four and no block is discarded, the proportion of genuine pairs rises to 80.8%.

Second-Stage Results: Are the Relevant Waits Less Likely?

I next examine whether staggered discards of terminal pairs are associated with a lower probability of inward suji waits and bad-shape waits on 5.

The three outcomes are:

  1. a wait on the first inward suji: 2–5 after staggered discards of 1, or 5–8 after staggered discards of 9;
  2. a wait on the second inward suji: 3–6 after discards of 1, or 4–7 after discards of 9; and
  3. a bad-shape wait on 5: a tanki, kanchan, or shanpon wait.

Among riichi hands with staggered terminal-pair discards—the treated cases—the probability of a wait on the first inward suji is 4.725%. The corresponding probability for the second inward suji is 4.099%, while the probability of a bad-shape wait on 5 is 1.319%.

I compare these probabilities with the corresponding risks under several control conditions. I define the risk ratio as the probability in the staggered-discard group divided by the probability in the control group. The theory therefore predicts a risk ratio below 1 for all three outcomes. The first inward suji

Contrary to the theory’s principal prediction, waits on the first inward suji are more common after staggered terminal-pair discards. Compared with all other riichi hands, a hand with staggered discards of a pair of 1 is approximately 22% more likely to have a 2–5 wait. The probability is 4.725% in the staggered-discard group, compared with 3.873% among all other riichi hands. This corresponds to an absolute increase of 0.852 percentage points.

The predicted relationship appears only when the comparison group consists of riichi hands in which the player discarded a terminal pair on consecutive turns. Relative to that narrow comparison group, staggered discards are associated with a reduction of approximately 3%. This, however, does not appear to be the comparison Zundamon has in mind when applying the theory.

The data therefore do not support the theory’s strongest prediction. In the main comparison, the first inward suji is more dangerous.

The second inward suji

The results for the second inward suji are more consistent with the theory, although the difference is small.

Compared with all other riichi hands, hands with staggered terminal-pair discards are approximately 5% less likely to have a wait on the second inward suji. The probability is 4.099% in the staggered-discard group, compared with 4.315% among all other riichi hands. This corresponds to an absolute reduction of 0.216 percentage points.

Whether a reduction of this size should affect a practical push–fold decision depends on the precision of the player’s existing risk assessment and the other information available. My conjecture is that a difference of this magnitude will rarely change the correct decision by itself.

Bad-shape waits on 5

The largest difference concerns the auxiliary prediction about bad-shape waits on 5. Staggered terminal-pair discards are associated with an approximately 20% reduction in the probability of these waits. Thus, the prediction Zundamon presents as auxiliary receives more support than either of the theory’s principal predictions about inward suji.

The results follow the expected pattern. As the interval becomes longer and the probability of a genuine pair declines, the relevant associations generally become weaker. The practical implication is that we should focus on staggered discards separated by relatively short intervals. Cases with intervals longer than four appear too noisy to provide a useful signal.

For cases with intervals between one and four, the results can be summarized as follows:

  • Waits on the first inward suji are more likely: an increase of approximately 19%.
  • Waits on the second inward suji are less likely, although the difference is relatively small: a decrease of approximately 9%.
  • Bad-shape waits on 5 are less likely: a decrease of approximately 21%.

These results suggest that shorter intervals strengthen the predictive signal, but not always in the direction proposed by the theory. In particular, restricting the analysis to shorter intervals does not rescue the prediction concerning the first inward suji.

Investigating the mechanism

So, why does the theory’s main prediction fail? One possibility is that the first-stage assumption is unreliable: apparent staggered pair discards often do not come from genuine pairs because the second copy was drawn only after the first was discarded. If so, the observed discard pattern would not tell us anything about the shape surrounding the terminal at the time of the first discard. A second possibility is that the proposed second-stage mechanism is incomplete. Even when the player initially held a genuine pair, the retained terminal may have served a purpose other than belonging to one of the connected shapes considered in the theory’s elimination process (i.e., Table 1).

To distinguish between these explanations, I repeated the analysis using only genuine pairs. Specifically, I restricted the treated group to cases in which the player already held the second copy of the terminal when discarding the first. Opponents ordinarily do not have access to this information. Even after observing the second terminal discard, they cannot know whether the player held both copies at the time of the first discard. This restricted analysis is therefore not a directly usable hand-reading method. Instead, it is a mechanism test: it asks whether the theory’s prediction would hold if an observer could identify genuine pairs perfectly.

Even under this hypothetical perfect-information scenario, the first inward suji remains more dangerous. The failure of the theory’s principal prediction therefore cannot be attributed solely to the imperfect relationship between observed staggered discards and genuine pairs. Instead, the results suggest that the theory’s elimination process is incomplete. Even when the staggered discards come from a genuine pair, there appear to be enough alternative hand-development paths to prevent the first inward suji from becoming safer overall.

To understand these alternative paths, I reviewed Tenhou records in which a player began with a genuine terminal pair, discarded one copy, retained the other for several turns, and ultimately declared riichi with a wait on the first inward suji. These examples suggest that the theory underestimates several possibilities: players may lock in bad-shape taatsu such as in 1-shanten or 2-shanten hands; they may retain otherwise inefficient shapes while pursuing sanshoku or another yaku; and they may keep a terminal as a floating tile simply because they have no better alternative.

Each pathway may be uncommon individually. Collectively, however, they appear frequent enough to undermine the theory’s prediction concerning the first inward suji. The proposed elimination process does not account for enough of the ways in which actual hands develop.

Conclusion

I began this analysis hoping to find statistical support for Zundamon’s theory, but the results were more complicated than I expected. The prediction Zundamon presents as the most reliable—the relative safety of the first inward suji—received the least support. In fact, the first inward suji was more dangerous after staggered discards of the adjacent terminal pair.

The prediction concerning the second inward suji performed better. Waits on that suji were less common, particularly when the interval between the two terminal discards was short. The size of the overall difference, however, was modest.

The strongest evidence concerned the auxiliary prediction that bad-shape waits on 5 become less likely. This result may have practical value when a ryanmen wait involving 5 can be ruled out. For example, suppose an opponent declares riichi after making staggered hand discards of a pair of , and is genbutsu against that opponent. Because the discarded rules out a – ryanmen wait, the remaining danger of consists of – and bad-shape waits. If you are deciding between discarding and to achieve tenpai, you may want to discard in this situation.

I still believe Zundamon’s lectures and commentary videos are extremely valuable as learning materials. As Zundamon explains in episode 48, enumerating possible hand patterns and eliminating those that are inconsistent with the available evidence is a valuable mental exercise. Even when a particular inference does not hold statistically, working through its logic can improve our understanding of how hands develop.

Online platforms also allow us to compare our in-game reads with the complete game record afterward. This provides an additional reason to review game records: we can ask not only whether our decisions were correct, but also whether our reconstruction of an opponent’s hand was accurate. In my experience, this kind of hand-reading evaluation is one area in which current AI-based review tools remain relatively limited.

Finally, my analysis may have omitted an important qualification or misunderstood some aspect of Zundamon’s theory. The operational definitions, comparison groups, and sample restrictions used here represent only one way to test its predictions. I therefore encourage other mahjong researchers to conduct alternative analyses and determine whether they reach similar conclusions. I would be genuinely pleased if further evidence showed that my conclusions were mistaken.

Methods

Treatment definition

The same player discards the same terminal (1 or 9) two or more times with at least one discard in between, the second copy comes from the hand, and no other player has declared riichi before the second discard. Discards made after the player’s own riichi cannot be hand discards by choice, so they are excluded.

Wait classification

Waits are classified by decomposing the hand shape. The 2–5 wait includes forms such as the 2345 nobetan, the 34567 three-sided wait, and 3444; a shanpon on 2 and 5 does not count. A tanki on 5 counts as a bad-shape wait on 5.

Stratification

All ratios are Mantel–Haenszel weighted across 10 strata: riichi turn (5 bins) × prior riichi by another player (2). Intervals are the 2.5–97.5 percentiles of a cluster bootstrap that resamples games (hanchan): 400 draws for Table 4, 300 for Tables 5 and 6.

Block drops

A block drop is a pair of discards strictly between the two terminal discards, both from the hand, forming either the same tile (a toitsu) or two tiles of the same suit one or two apart (a taatsu). The two discards need not fall on consecutive turns.

Multiple testing

This is an exploratory analysis, so no adjustment is made. The intervals are nominal 95% intervals.

Limitations

These are observational comparisons, not experiments. The stratification covers only riichi turn and prior riichi, so other differences between the groups remain. And the outcome is which wait the hand ended with, not whether a particular tile deals in—a related but different quantity from actual deal-in rates.

However, they matter if the narrower goal is to isolate the predictive contribution of the staggered discard itself. ↩
  5. An interval length is defined as the number of intervening turns. For example, when the first discard is discarded on the fourth turn, and the second is discarded on the sixth turn, the interval is one. ↩
