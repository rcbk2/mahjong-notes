The Sign of Four (Sets)

Is tedashi really a signal of tenpai?

14 September 2026EnglishLog analysisDiscard readingTenhou

rcbk2The beast that counted tiles at the edge of the world.

TL;DR

  • A hand discard (tedashi) should lower—not raise—your estimate of an open-hand opponent’s tenpai probability.

Hand Discards after a Call

The previous analysis examined a folk theorem about an open-hand opponent’s probability of being in tenpai at the moment of a call. The data supported the so-called 3–2–1 rule: when an opponent makes their third call in row one, their second call in row two, or their first call in row three, their hand is in tenpai roughly 50% of the time.

But how should we estimate their probability of being in tenpai afterward? Hirasawa argues that we should revise our estimate upward when an open-hand opponent discards from the hand (tedashi) rather than from the draw (tsumogiri) after their most recent call.1 When discussing the 3–2–1 rule, Hirasawa almost always adds this as a supplementary insight.2

The prediction sounds quite intuitive, perhaps even obvious. When a player draws a useful tile that advances their hand, they keep it and discard another tile from their hand. By contrast, when they draw a useless tile, they are likely to discard the tile they just drew. The usual story, then, is that observing a hand discard should lead us to revise the opponent’s tenpai probability upward, whereas observing a draw discard should leave it unchanged.

Paradoxically, however, the opposite may sometimes be true. Observing a hand discard may require us to revise our estimate of an opponent’s tenpai probability downward.

To see how this is possible, consider the following simple formal model. Suppose we do not know an opponent’s shanten status, but our initial estimate is that their hand is in tenpai 50% of the time, 1-shanten 30% of the time, 2-shanten 15% of the time, and 3-shanten or worse 5% of the time (the numbers are chosen for illustration). Assume that when the player draws a useless tile—that is, one that does not improve the hand’s shanten—they discard it immediately. If they draw a useful tile while already in tenpai, they win by tsumo and leave our analytic sample. If they draw a useful tile while their hand is k-shanten, where k = 1, 2, 3, …, they keep the drawn tile and discard a tile from the hand. Finally, assume that the probability of drawing a useful tile depends on the hand’s shanten status. When the hand is in tenpai (k = 0), the probability of drawing a winning tile is 5%. For k = 1, assume that the probability of drawing a useful tile is 10%; for k = 2, 20%; and for k ≥ 3, 40%. This simplification is broadly reasonable: hands closer to completion generally accept fewer tiles.

Under this model, observing a hand discard lowers our estimate of the player’s tenpai probability from 50% to 37.5%. By contrast, observing a draw discard raises it to approximately 53.1%.3

The prediction generated by this simple model appears consistent with some previous statistical analyses of tenpai, tedashi, and tsumogiri. For example, Amber@Path of Houou found that, among closed hands, more tsumogiri, not more tedashi, predicts tenpai.

We therefore have two competing mechanisms: tedashi may indicate that the hand has just advanced, while tsumogiri may indicate that the hand was already in tenpai. Our task is to put these competing predictions to an empirical test using data on open hands. More specifically, Hirasawa’s conjecture predicts that the probability of tenpai after a tedashi is higher than the probability after a tsumogiri. The formal model above suggests that the opposite may hold. Let us see what the data say.

Tenpai Probabilities after Tedashi and Tsumogiri

I treat hands whose first call occurred on turn k (for k = 3, 6, 9, or 12) as a cohort. For each subsequent turn t (t = k + m, …, 18), I then calculate the probability of tenpai after the mth hand discard following the call (for m = 1, 2, or 3). In the figures below, the blue curves show the tenpai probability when the first hand discard occurs on turn t, the yellow curves show it when the second hand discard occurs on turn t, and the red curves show it when the third hand discard occurs on turn t. The gray curves show the baseline tenpai probability on turn t for all hands in the cohort, including those that have not made a hand discard since the call. Observations leave the cohort once the player calls again or the hand ends (by any player’s win or an exhaustive draw). Curves are omitted for cases with fewer than 200 observations.

The baseline probabilities mostly level off after five or six turns because tenpai hands leave the denominator when they win. The saturation level therefore depends on the turn of the most recent call.

Remarkably, the colored curves fall below the gray baseline in many cases. This suggests that, after observing an open-hand player make a hand discard, we should adjust our estimate of their tenpai probability downward rather than upward. For hands with a very early first call (k = 3), the number of subsequent hand discards is mostly noise: the tenpai probabilities differ little through roughly turn 10, and only modestly thereafter. For hands with a later first call (k = 6, 9, or 12), the number of hand discards begins to matter, but in a counterintuitive way. In all three cohorts, a greater number of hand discards between the most recent call and turn t is associated with a lower tenpai probability (m = 1 > m = 2 > m = 3). These differences become larger as k increases. Among hands whose first call occurred on turn 12, those that subsequently made three consecutive hand discards through turn 15 had only a 14.3% chance of being in tenpai, less than one-third of the baseline probability.

The blue curves (m = 1) do appear to cross above the gray baseline toward the end of the hand. As we will see in the next section, however, this is because the baseline probability is not conditioned on the number of previous hand discards.

Figures for n = 2 cohorts

The relationship is even clearer among cohorts defined by a second call. Because the baseline tenpai probabilities are already so high, the probabilities associated with hand discards (m = 1, 2, or 3) never exceed the baseline, except on turn 18 for k = 6. For hands with two calls, the timing of the second call is a stronger signal of tenpai than the number of subsequent hand discards. As long as the player continues to discard from their hand, their tenpai probability remains lower than the baseline.

Figures for n = 3 cohorts

The relationship is clearest among cohorts defined by a third call. The baseline tenpai probabilities are even higher, reaching 81–83% toward the end, so tedashi curves sit far below it.

As the table shows, the tenpai probability is even lower when a player discards a block from their hand. This finding is consistent with the observations of expert hand-reading theorists such as Akihiro Kawamura. He writes, “An open hand has a low probability of being in tenpai at the moment the player completes a protorun discard” (鳴き手の人が手出しでターツ落としを完了させた瞬間はテンパイ率が低い). Zundamon has made similar remarks in their commentary—for example, in episode 48 and episode 68.

Conditioning on History

Although the figures above are descriptively accurate, they do not directly answer the question we face while defending: should we adjust our estimate based on whether the opponent makes a hand discard or a draw discard? The hand-discard groups (m = 1, 2, or 3) are restricted to hands with m − 1 previous hand discards following the most recent call, whereas the baseline group has no such restriction. Because the defending player knows how many previous hand discards they have observed, the appropriate comparison is between hand discards and draw discards while controlling for the number of previous hand discards (r). The results are strikingly consistent. In every cohort, turn, and history level examined, hands with a hand discard have a lower probability of being in tenpai than hands with a draw discard. A defender who knows the number of previous hand discards should therefore adjust their estimate of the opponent’s tenpai probability downward after a hand discard and upward after a draw discard. This makes intuitive sense: players who continue making draw discards toward the end of a hand are more likely to have already reached tenpai.

  3. To see why, we can apply Bayes’ theorem, updating our prior belief in light of the newly observed discard. Under the model, a player who begins the turn in tenpai will either win by tsumo or draw-discard; they will never hand-discard. Thus, the players who are in tenpai immediately after a hand discard are precisely those who began the turn at 1-shanten and drew a useful tile:

    0.30 × 0.10 = 0.03.

    The denominator consists of all players who hand-discard: those who began at 1-shanten, 2-shanten, or 3-shanten or worse and then drew a useful tile:

    (0.30 × 0.10) + (0.15 × 0.20) + (0.05 × 0.40) = 0.08.

    The resulting conditional probability is therefore

    0.030.08 = 37.5%.

    A draw discard, by assumption, does not change the player’s shanten status. Players who remain in tenpai after draw-discarding are therefore those who were already in tenpai at the beginning of the turn, excluding the 5% who drew a winning tile:

    0.50 × (1 − 0.05) = 0.475.

    The overall probability of observing a draw discard is

    (0.50 × 0.95) + (0.30 × 0.90) + (0.15 × 0.80) + (0.05 × 0.60) = 0.895.

    Thus, conditional on observing a draw discard, the estimated probability of tenpai is

    0.4750.895 ≈ 53.1%. ↩
